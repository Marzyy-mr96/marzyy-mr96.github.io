## The question

Disaster recovery does not end when the water recedes or the roof is repaired. For many households, returning to stable, functioning housing takes years, and progress is uneven: some families recover quickly while others stay displaced or in partially repaired homes long after the event. When a second disaster arrives before recovery from the first is complete, those gaps can widen.

Studying this requires data that follow the same homes and households for many years. Such data are rare, and they describe people whose privacy must be protected.

## Where AI and data science fit

Machine learning can reveal patterns in long-term recovery that are difficult to see otherwise, such as the different pathways households follow over time. But disaster data are small, incomplete, and collected under difficult conditions, so AI can also mislead. My research applies end-to-end data science, from integrating years of field records to modeling and validation, and uses AI where it adds insight while examining it critically: whether its validation is honest, whether it treats different communities fairly, and whether it would hold for the next disaster. Statistical models then help explain the patterns that AI reveals, and my background in uncertainty quantification shapes how I judge the strength of every result.

## The study

The research builds on the NIST-funded longitudinal field study of Lumberton, North Carolina, a community flooded by Hurricane Matthew in October 2016 and again by Hurricane Florence in September 2018. Since 2016, a multi-disciplinary research team has returned to Lumberton in repeated field-study waves to document how housing, households, businesses, and infrastructure recover. My doctoral research is advised by Dr. Elaina J. Sutley at the University of Kansas.
