Research / Disaster Resilience

Disaster Resilience

AI and Long-Term Disaster Recovery

Ph.D. research on how households and housing recover over many years after repeated flooding, using machine learning and statistical modeling with careful attention to when AI can be trusted.

Status
Ongoing Ph.D. research
Tools
  • Python
  • scikit-learn
  • statsmodels
  • pandas

The question

Disaster recovery does not end when the water recedes or the roof is repaired. For many households, returning to stable, functioning housing takes years, and progress is uneven: some families recover quickly while others stay displaced or in partially repaired homes long after the event. When a second disaster arrives before recovery from the first is complete, those gaps can widen.

Studying this requires data that follow the same homes and households for many years. Such data are rare, and they describe people whose privacy must be protected.

Where AI and data science fit

Machine learning can reveal patterns in long-term recovery that are difficult to see otherwise, such as the different pathways households follow over time. But disaster data are small, incomplete, and collected under difficult conditions, so AI can also mislead. My research applies end-to-end data science, from integrating years of field records to modeling and validation, and uses AI where it adds insight while examining it critically: whether its validation is honest, whether it treats different communities fairly, and whether it would hold for the next disaster. Statistical models then help explain the patterns that AI reveals, and my background in uncertainty quantification shapes how I judge the strength of every result.

The study

The research builds on the NIST-funded longitudinal field study of Lumberton, North Carolina, a community flooded by Hurricane Matthew in October 2016 and again by Hurricane Florence in September 2018. Since 2016, a multi-disciplinary research team has returned to Lumberton in repeated field-study waves to document how housing, households, businesses, and infrastructure recover. My doctoral research is advised by Dr. Elaina J. Sutley at the University of Kansas.

Research workstreams

01Data integration

Following recovery over the long term

Following the same households over several years requires a consistent record of their recovery, even as survey questions and participation change. I reconcile household and housing information across survey waves, document how inconsistent or missing responses are handled, and retain unresolved values when the evidence does not support an inference. This creates a traceable foundation for examining recovery over time and communicating it through tables, figures, and reports.

  • Python
  • pandas
  • NumPy
  • openpyxl

02Unsupervised learning

Recognizing recovery pathways with AI

Households can reach similar recovery levels through very different experiences, so I examine how their trajectories unfold rather than relying only on average trends. I use Dynamic Time Warping with hierarchical clustering to group observed trajectories and latent class growth analysis to explore model-based pathways. Comparing the two approaches and assessing the separation and fit of the resulting groups helps me interpret which patterns are supported by the data.

  • Python
  • NumPy
  • SciPy
  • scikit-learn

03Statistical modeling

Understanding why recovery is unequal

To investigate differences in recovery, I combine nonparametric group comparisons with regression and linear mixed-effects models. This lets me examine demographic and housing characteristics while accounting for repeated observations of the same households. I distinguish unadjusted differences from adjusted associations and use sensitivity analysis to consider how analytical choices affect the interpretation. The aim is to describe unequal recovery without treating observational associations as proof of causation.

  • Python
  • statsmodels
  • SciPy

04Machine learning evaluation

Trustworthy AI for recovery decisions

Predictive models need careful evaluation before their results can inform recovery decisions. I compare Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and regularized logistic regression with simple baselines, using validation that keeps related household records together. Diagnosing data leakage has been an important part of this work. I examine discrimination, calibration, and feature importance to understand both model performance and the limits of a prediction.

  • Python
  • scikit-learn
  • pandas

05Geospatial analytics

Recovery in place

Household recovery also reflects the places in which people live. I bring public hazard, census, and social vulnerability information into the analysis and use spatial statistics to explore geographic patterns. Documenting the sources and limitations of these contextual data helps me interpret how neighborhood conditions relate to household observations, while keeping exact household locations and identifying information within private analyses.

  • Python
  • NumPy
  • pandas
  • public geospatial data
